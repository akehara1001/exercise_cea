- v6［paragraph］Kaplan-Meier curves of KEYNOTE-522 were digitized from the published figures: event-free survival by arm and pathological complete response (pCR) status at interim analysis 4, with a data cutoff of 23 March 20214, and overall survival by arm at the final analysis of overall survival, with a data cutoff of 22 March 20245. - v6［paragraph］Event-free survival after surgery
- v6［paragraph］Event-free survival was modeled from surgery, set at 7 months after randomization, separately for the four combinations of arm and pCR status. The candidate models were six standard parametric distributions, six Royston-Parmar spline models and three mixture cure models on the relative survival scale, with background mortality from the Japanese life table for women51. A model was eligible if at least 95% of the monthly points with at least 20 patients at risk lay within the pointwise 95% confidence interval of the Kaplan-Meier estimate, if its excess hazard peaked within 3 years after surgery and declined thereafter, and if its all-cause hazard at 10 and 20 years was not below background mortality.
- v6［paragraph］Within each pCR stratum, both arms used the same distribution family. Among the families eligible in both arms whose summed Akaike information criterion was within 4 of the lowest, the family whose 5-year extrapolation was closest to the 5-year event-free survival reported at a later data cutoff was selected43. This gave a Weibull model for patients with pCR and a Weibull mixture cure model for patients without pCR. Event-free survival at 7 years in each stratum was then fixed at the value of the final analysis44 (Supplementary Table S2), by maximizing the likelihood subject to this equality.
- v6［paragraph］Events before surgery, first events after surgery and deaths from adverse events
- v6［paragraph］Before surgery, event-free survival in each stratum followed its Kaplan-Meier estimate, and patients with pCR had no events before surgery. When the reported proportion exceeded the decline of that estimate, the excess was placed in the first cycle after surgery and taken from the excess hazard, so the total was unchanged.
- v6［paragraph］First events after surgery were divided into locoregional recurrence, including second primary cancers, distant recurrence and death without recurrence, in the proportions of the 2024 report5. Locoregional recurrence progressed to distant recurrence at a constant rate4. Deaths from adverse events, in the numbers reported for each arm4, occurred at a constant hazard during the 12 months of treatment and were moved within the fitted event-free survival without changing it.
- v6［paragraph］Survival after recurrence and background mortality
- v6［paragraph］Overall survival curves of patients with early and late relapse in the Epidemiological Strategy and Medical Economics (ESME) cohort50 were digitized and reconstructed in the same way, and log-logistic distributions were fitted to them. A common shape was used because the shapes of the two groups did not differ significantly. Excess mortality after distant recurrence within 18 months of randomization followed the early-relapse distribution and after later recurrence the late-relapse distribution, in the same way in all arms. Excess mortality after progression that precluded surgery followed the early-relapse distribution.
- v6［paragraph］Survival after locoregional recurrence carried background mortality only. Background mortality came from the 2025 abridged life table for Japanese women51, from age 49 years. The all-cause hazard was never below background mortality; where a fitted model for patients with pCR fell below it late in follow-up, background mortality applied.
- v6［paragraph］Validation
- v6［paragraph］Overall survival was not used to fit or calibrate any parameter. The model was accepted if three criteria, fixed before the comparison, were met. First, overall survival of each arm at years 1 to 5 lay within the pointwise 95% confidence intervals of the reconstructed Kaplan-Meier estimates, or all ten points lay within Bonferroni-adjusted 99.5% intervals. Second, the 5-year difference in restricted mean survival time between arms lay within the bootstrap 95% interval of the Kaplan-Meier difference. Third, overall and event-free survival at 7 years lay within the 95% confidence intervals of the final analysis44. Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table S3 show the comparison.
- v6［paragraph］Probabilistic sensitivity analysis
- v6［paragraph］Each of the 1,000 probabilistic samples combined one bootstrap replicate of the survival model with one draw of the other inputs. In each replicate, the reconstructed event-free survival data were resampled within arm together with their pCR status and the models were refitted with the 7-year equality; the 7-year values were drawn from binomial distributions with the stratum sizes of interim analysis 4. The reconstructed ESME data were resampled within group and the log-logistic distributions refitted.
- v6［paragraph］The other inputs were drawn independently from the distributions in Supplementary Table S2: gamma distributions for costs, durations and events per patient, beta distributions for proportions and utilities, and lognormal distributions for hazard ratios, with parameters derived from the 95% ranges. Drug prices and rates based on zero events were fixed, and the duration of the utility decrement for adverse events and the discount rate were not varied. Every sample can be reproduced from its random seed (base seed 20261023, plus the sample number for the bootstrap). The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles across samples give the 95% uncertainty intervals.

### manuscript 块 2（改）
- v5［paragraph］The evidence needed to judge exercise during neoadjuvant chemotherapy lacks two things. First, economic evaluations of exercise in cancer have valued effects measured in trials, mostly on fatigue, fitness or quality of life during or after adjuvant treatment20–22. In breast cancer, such evaluations of exercise during adjuvant chemotherapy reached different conclusions23–25. When the effect on recurrence is unknown, a cost-effectiveness ratio cannot be estimated, but the highest program cost that remains cost-effective can be calculated for each possible effect26. Second, cost-effectiveness analyses of the KEYNOTE-522 regimen came from the United States, Hong Kong, Switzerland and Egypt27–30. The Japanese analyses identified concerned pembrolizumab for advanced cancers, including metastatic TNBC31–37. As of October 2026, pembrolizumab had not been designated for Japan's cost-effectiveness evaluation38. Without a Japanese estimate for pembrolizumab, there is no reference against which the cost and health gain of exercise can be judged.
- v5［paragraph］This study aimed to estimate, from the perspective of the Japanese public healthcare payer, the maximum cost per patient at which adding a structured exercise program to neoadjuvant chemotherapy with pembrolizumab for stage II or III triple-negative breast cancer would be cost-effective, and how this cost depends on the size and duration of the effect of exercise on recurrence. The secondary aims were to estimate the same cost when exercise is added to neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone, to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of pembrolizumab in Japan as a reference, and to estimate the effect of exercise that would yield the same gain in quality-adjusted life years as pembrolizumab. No economic evaluation of exercise during neoadjuvant therapy for breast cancer was identified in searches of PubMed and Europe PMC covering 2000 to 2026, last run on 26 October 2026. The nearest, Phys-Can, compared two exercise intensities during neoadjuvant or adjuvant treatment of breast, colorectal or prostate cancer39.

### manuscript 块 8（增）
- v6［paragraph］Discussion
- v6［paragraph］The value of adding a structured exercise program to neoadjuvant chemotherapy for stage II or III TNBC in Japan depends on how much and for how long the program lowers the risk of recurrence. With chemotherapy plus pembrolizumab, a program could cost up to JPY 2,726 thousand per patient at a recurrence HR of 0.85 and remain cost-effective (Table 2). The most expensive design costed in this study was JPY 239,508 (Table 1). A reduction of 1.1% in the recurrence hazard was enough for the fee-schedule-based program to be cost-effective, and a reduction of 4.2% to 8.6% was enough for it to lower total medical costs (Table 2). For patients in Japan, what remains unknown is whether exercise lowers recurrence at all, and for how long.
- v6［paragraph］Most of this value comes from distant recurrences that the program would prevent. Treatment after distant recurrence in Japan now costs JPY 1,215,933 to 1,458,318 per month in first-line treatment and JPY 1,412,953 per month in later lines (Table 1). Medical costs avoided made up JPY 702 thousand of the maximum affordable cost at an HR of 0.85 (Supplementary Table S6). Lower metastatic treatment costs reduced these savings but left the threshold for cost-effectiveness almost unchanged. With these costs halved, or with eribulin-based later-line treatment, the break-even HR was 0.988 and 0.987, while the HR at which exercise lowered total costs fell to 0.918 and 0.914 (Table 2).
- v6［paragraph］How long the effect lasts changed the result more than any single cost. If the effect faded from 1 year after surgery, the maximum affordable cost at an HR of 0.85 fell to JPY 1,772 thousand, and if it did not wane, it rose to JPY 3,404 thousand (Table 2). In the one-way analyses, no input moved this cost outside JPY 2,617 thousand to 2,836 thousand (Fig. 3). After neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy, most recurrences occur within 24 months of randomization6, so an effect that lasts through the first years after surgery carries most of the value. Continuing exercise after surgery raised the maximum affordable cost when the effect would otherwise have faded early (Supplementary Table S7).
- v6［paragraph］For pembrolizumab, the ICER of JPY 2,053 thousand per QALY lay below both Japanese thresholds (Table 3). Analyses of the same regimen from the United States, Hong Kong, Switzerland and Egypt28–31 found it cost-effective, with ICERs below the threshold used in each setting. In Japan, the regimen has not been designated for cost-effectiveness evaluation39. Its ICER rose to JPY 3,044 thousand per QALY when capecitabine or olaparib was added after surgery for patients without pCR, as in Japanese practice, and fell to JPY 1,296 thousand at 75% of the list price of pembrolizumab (Table 3; Supplementary Fig. S2).
- v6［paragraph］Exercise cannot stand in for pembrolizumab. To gain as many QALYs as pembrolizumab, exercise added to chemotherapy alone would need a recurrence HR of 0.425 (Table 3), a far larger effect than has been reported for exercise in other settings9,14. The maximum affordable cost was higher when exercise was added to chemotherapy alone (Table 2), because more recurrences remain for exercise to prevent without pembrolizumab. Exercise is therefore best judged as an addition to the standard regimen, which in Japan was given to 2,294 of 3,451 patients in 20247.
- v6［paragraph］Previous economic evaluations of exercise in cancer valued effects on fatigue, fitness or quality of life during or shortly after treatment20–22. The cost-utility analysis of CHALLENGE valued an effect on disease-free survival over the long term46. The structured exercise program was less costly and more effective than health education materials, and cancer recurrence or new malignancy and anticancer therapy were the major cost drivers46. Our results agree with it on the main point: once a program changes the risk of recurrence, its value reflects the cost and the loss of health that each prevented recurrence avoids, and both are large relative to the cost of the program. Headroom analysis is used to judge technologies before their effect is known27. Here it shows that an effect far smaller than a trial could detect would still pay for the program.
- v6［paragraph］In Japanese practice, the value of a given effect may be larger than modeled. In the 2024 national registry, 47% of patients treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy had a pCR7, fewer than in either arm of KEYNOTE-522 (63.0% and 55.6%; Table 1). More patients therefore carry residual disease and the higher risk of recurrence on which exercise would act. Even at an HR of 0.95, the net benefit for one year of Japanese patients would be JPY 2.68 billion (Supplementary Table S8).
- v6［paragraph］Implications
- v6［paragraph］Trials of exercise during neoadjuvant chemotherapy for TNBC should measure recurrence and follow patients long enough to show whether any effect persists after the program ends. A higher pCR would help but cannot settle the question alone. In this model, 1 percentage point more pCR was worth JPY 389 thousand per patient (Supplementary Table S5), but how a change in pCR caused by exercise would translate into fewer recurrences is unknown. Given the net benefit at stake for each year of patients in Japan (Supplementary Table S8), such trials are a sound use of research funds. For payers, the fee-schedule price of a supervised program is far below what a confirmed effect would justify, and support that keeps patients exercising after surgery, such as remote or app-based programs, would add value if it extends the effect (Supplementary Table S7). For pembrolizumab, the analysis provides a Japanese estimate for a regimen that has not been designated for cost-effectiveness evaluation.
- v6［paragraph］Limitations
- v6［paragraph］The effect of exercise during neoadjuvant chemotherapy on recurrence is unknown, so all results for exercise are conditional on an assumed HR and duration of effect. The CHALLENGE estimate used as a reference comes from a 3-year program after adjuvant chemotherapy for colon cancer. The model credited exercise only with fewer recurrences after surgery; effects on fatigue, quality of life or completion of chemotherapy were not counted, so the maximum affordable cost may be understated. Survival was modeled from patient-level data reconstructed from published curves, and the 7-year event-free survival by pCR status came from the presentation of the final analysis rather than a full report. Survival after distant recurrence came from a French cohort treated largely before immunotherapy and antibody–drug conjugates were available, whereas treatment costs reflect regimens used in 2026. Several costs relied on clinical expert input, and ranges were set by rule where sources gave none (Fig. 3). Utilities used the German value set; values from Japanese populations gave similar results (Supplementary Table S10). The number of eligible patients came from a registry that does not capture every case.
