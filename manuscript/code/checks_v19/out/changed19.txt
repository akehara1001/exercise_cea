Trial inputs came from KEYNOTE-522 reports with data cutoffs from March 2021 to October 2025 [4,5,43,44]. The analysis plan, and each later addition, was written before the corresponding computation; the plan was not registered. The study follows the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 (CHEERS 2022) [45]. Supplementary Methods S1, Tables S1–S12 (including the CHEERS checklist in Table S1) and Figs. S1–S2 are in Online Resource 1.
Each program was assumed to deliver supervised aerobic and resistance training twice weekly during the 6 months of neoadjuvant chemotherapy; the programs differed only in cost. The supervised program assigned the whole CHALLENGE program cost [46], converted from 2024 Canadian dollars by purchasing power parity [47], to these 6 months, because its roughly 50 sessions over 3 years match twice-weekly sessions over 6 months. The fee-schedule-based program priced 40 minutes of outpatient musculoskeletal rehabilitation twice weekly for 26 weeks, because rehabilitation for patients with cancer is reimbursed only during admission, and the remote program one face-to-face session, monthly online visits and an app [48].
Table 2 also shows pembrolizumab against chemotherapy alone: it cost JPY 4,426 thousand more per patient and added 2.156 QALYs, an ICER of JPY 2,053 thousand per QALY (Table S4). To gain as many QALYs as pembrolizumab, exercise added to chemotherapy alone required a recurrence HR of 0.425 or an increase in pCR of 35.9 percentage points (Table 2). To give the same net monetary benefit, it required an HR of 0.722 at JPY 5 million per QALY and 0.638 at JPY 7.5 million, or increases in pCR of 19.0 and 24.1 percentage points. Except the HR of 0.722, these values lay outside the ranges studied.
If exercise lowered the recurrence hazard by 5% or more, both strategies without it were dominated (Tables 2 and 3), so the choice in Japan is not between exercise and pembrolizumab. Pembrolizumab remained cost-effective on top of exercise, as it is on its own in Japan and elsewhere [28–31], and chemotherapy with both gave the most health for the money at every effect size.
Exercise cannot replace pembrolizumab in health gained: it would need to lower the recurrence hazard by more than half, far beyond the effects reported in exercise trials [9,14]. In value for money the gap is narrower, because the program costs a small fraction of the pembrolizumab regimen: at the Japanese threshold, an effect the size of that in CHALLENGE would give the same net benefit as pembrolizumab, and at the higher threshold pembrolizumab keeps its lead unless exercise cuts the hazard by more than a third. Because the effect of exercise is unproven, the comparison shows what a cheap program could be worth if it were confirmed.
For patients who receive pembrolizumab, now most patients in Japan [7], the question is what a program added to their treatment may cost. If exercise reduces recurrence at all, even a small effect would make a structured program cost-effective: the fee-schedule-based program became cost-effective once it lowered the recurrence hazard by 1.1%, which cut distant recurrence at 7 years by about 0.14 percentage points (Table S12). What a program could cost depended on the size and duration of its effect.
